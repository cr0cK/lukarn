# 1.3.0 — An account can be a person

**Target.** An account may be bound to a verified identity. Bound, it is one
person: they are known as soon as their session opens, they sign comments without
proving their address again on every device, and they sign in with a code sent to
that address instead of a password. Unbound, it is the shared access key of 1.2 and
nothing about it changes.

Two decisions carry it, stated below and **not written to `08-decisions/` yet**:
the binding rather than a second authentication mode, and the code rather than a
password. They are settled and not open for relitigation here — what remains open
is only where they live, and a decision log describes the application as it is,
which neither of them is of any running instance. The pull request that implements
each writes it, with the identifier of the day it is written. This is what the
storage work did before it.

Delivered as **two successive pull requests**. PR 1 is not releasable on its own —
it builds the API and no screen reaches it — which is the same shape PR 1 of the
storage work had.

## What already exists and is not to be rebuilt

Most of this work is wiring, because the parts were built for commenting.

- **`commenters`** holds the address, the display name, the notification switch and
  the language (`locale`), plus the code apparatus D39 put there: `code_hash` as an
  HMAC, `code_expires_at`, `code_sent_at`, `code_attempts`. The rules those columns
  enforce are reused unchanged; **the columns themselves move** to a table of their
  own in PR 1, leaving `commenters` as identity alone (the sign-in decision).
- **`throttle.ts`** already blocks on three axes and is already reused by device
  pairing with the code standing in for the username. The new routes join it the
  same way; there is no fourth counter to add.
- **`plugins/auth.ts` rereads the identity on every request** and detaches it when
  it has disappeared. Reading it from the account instead of the session is a
  change of source in one place, not a new mechanism.
- **`mail.ts` composes and queues**, and `buildVerificationMail` is the template
  the two new messages follow.
- **`ConfigRepo` keeps an in-memory snapshot** of accounts and albums, and it is
  authoritative. Anything added to `users` goes through it, never through a direct
  `UPDATE` in the same process.

## The two decisions this work takes

Written here rather than in `08-decisions/` until the code exists. Each moves into
a file of its own, under the date it is written, in the pull request that makes it
true.

### One axis, not a second authentication mode

`users` is an access key. [D38](../08-decisions/D38-an-access-key-is-not-a-person.md)
separated it from `commenters`, the person who signs a comment, because a password
given to a whole family cannot say who is writing. That answers the family album
and leaves one case uncovered: somebody outside the household who should hold an
account of their own, whose name follows them from a phone to a laptop without
being proved again on each, and to whom nobody wants to hand the shared password.

The shape that suggests itself is an instance-wide setting — shared keys here,
personal accounts there, each with its own sign-in. **That is the shape this
refuses.** `users.commenter_id` binds an account to a verified identity, or it is
`NULL` and nothing changes.

| The account is | What it is                         | Where the identity comes from    |
| -------------- | ---------------------------------- | -------------------------------- |
| Unbound        | An access key, shareable as before | The session, declared per device |
| Bound          | One person                         | The account, on every request    |

`user_albums` remains the only source of album permissions, keyed by
`users.username`, and that is the point of doing it this way.
[D33](../08-decisions/D33-no-google-sign-in-for-commenting.md) rejected Google
sign-in for visitors because a Google identity exists in neither `users` nor
`user_albums`: honouring it would have required an allowlist of addresses per
album, which is `users` rewritten under another name. A binding adds a person to an
account that already carries permissions. It creates no second population, and
every rule written against a session — `canSee()` on every request, 404 and never
403, the account reread on every request — stays true without ever asking which
kind of account it is looking at.

**Why not a mode.** Two modes are two security models to hold at once, and
[04](../04-security-and-access.md) is written for one. Much of it is justified by
the sharing itself: pairing a screen delegates an access key and deliberately does
not carry the approver's identity
([D260809c](../08-decisions/D260809c-a-television-does-not-type-a-password-it-displays.md));
moderation's remedy for a key that has circulated too widely is to change its
password; the visit counters stop at the album because counting photo by photo
would produce a viewing history in an application where a household shares one
password. Under a second mode every one of those sentences needs its other half —
in the code, in the tests, and on every page that states them.

**The binding is proved by the address, never asserted from /admin.** An
administrator able to point an account at an existing identity could point their
own account at anyone who has ever commented and sign as them: the impersonation
[D39](../08-decisions/D39-the-address-is-verified-by-a-one-time-code.md) exists to
prevent, moved from the comment form to the accounts table.

**Rejected.** _An instance-wide authentication mode_, above; it also fails on its
own terms, since the two populations coexist in one household — the living-room
television wants a shared key at the same moment as the cousin who comments wants
an account. _Permissions carried by `commenters`_: it splits "may this session see
this album" across two tables, exactly the join `canSee()` exists not to perform on
every thumbnail. _The administrator binding an existing identity from /admin_: the
convenience is real and it costs an impersonation no audit trail would undo. _The
address as the sign-in identifier in place of `username`_: D38 rejected it because
`users.username` is referenced by `user_albums` and `comments` without
`ON UPDATE CASCADE`, and the binding is what makes it unnecessary.

**Consequences beyond the checklists.** The pairing rule inverts, and only for a
bound account: [04](../04-security-and-access.md) states that the commenter
identity does not follow a paired screen, because on a shared key the approver is
one of several people; on a bound account the identity _is_ the account, so it
follows by construction, and approving a screen delegates the ability to sign as
you. **The visit counters do not change** — what changes is their justification,
from "the counters cannot identify a person" to "the counters do not identify a
person", which is the stronger promise; recorded so that a later session does not
read personal accounts as permission to count photo by photo. **`comments.account`
keeps its meaning**, and on a bound account names that person's own key; the remedy
it was added for has no equivalent there, and deleting the account is what closes
that door. **A key genuinely shared by a household is retired, not converted** —
conversion is for the account that was only ever one person, and the living-room
television keeps a shared account with no identity, which is the case the unbound
half exists to preserve. **Registration stays excluded**
([01](../01-vision-and-scope.md)): what the invitation changes is who types the
secret, not who may open the door.

### The code is the credential, and a bound account holds no password

A password drags in the one thing [01](../01-vision-and-scope.md) lists as out of
scope beside registration: forgotten passwords. An instance run by a family member
is precisely the instance where nobody wants to be the reset desk, and a reset flow
is a token, an expiry, an email and a page — the whole surface the project avoided
by having the owner create accounts. Meanwhile the instance already sends a code to
an address and checks it back (D39: six digits, an HMAC, fifteen minutes, five
attempts, one send per minute).

So a bound account signs in by entering a code sent to its address, and **the code
machinery leaves `commenters` for a table of its own**. D39 put those four columns
there when there was one thing to prove; a second use turns them into one column
set with two meanings, which is the shape that rots. What is left on `commenters`
is what a person is. The extraction also names what the two tables now are: `users`
carries authorisation, `commenters` carries identity, and a code is neither.

**A bound account cannot be given a password.** Allowing both and letting each
account choose hands an administrator a way through the front door of an identity
they were forbidden to assert: set a password on a bound account, sign in, and the
session signs as that person. Losing access to the address is recovered by
unbinding, which closes the sessions and requires a password in the same request —
the administrator taking the account back and handing it over as a shared key,
which is what it has become. The refusal belongs to `ConfigRepo`, not to the route:
`pnpm reset-password` writes through the same repository without passing any route.

**Rejected.** _A password with a reset flow_: the address is already verified and
already receives mail, so the reset was going to be a code sent to it — the code is
the credential and the reset disappears with the password. _A clickable link
instead of a code_: D39 rejected it for comment identity and the reason is stronger
for sign-in, since a link opens the session in whichever browser is the system
default, not the one holding the screen open. _Keeping the code columns on
`commenters` with a purpose flag_: one pending code per address, so verifying an
address while signing in overwrites one with the other, and the fourth purpose this
design anticipates arrives with nowhere to sit. _A nullable `password_hash`_:
SQLite cannot relax `NOT NULL` in place, so it would mean rebuilding `users` to
express with a null what a reserved hash expresses without touching the schema.
_Sign-in through an external provider, now_: the obstacle is not the one usually
assumed — `drive.readonly` is a restricted scope and its audit is what
[D46](../08-decisions/D46-a-service-account-to-stop-seeing-google-hasn-t-verified.md)
avoids, but `openid email profile` is not restricted and carries no such audit. The
obstacles are that every self-hoster would still register a client of their own,
and that 01 promises a visitor never sees a Google URL. If an external provider is
ever wanted the shape is **generic OIDC**, with Google one provider beside
Authelia, Authentik and Keycloak, binding a credential to an account that was
invited and never creating one.

**Consequences beyond the checklists.** These accounts **require SMTP**, as
commenting already does. The residual attack is social and no secret closes it —
someone triggers a code to an address and talks its holder into reading it out — so
the email states what the code grants in its subject and its first line, and the
purposes are separated so a code obtained for one cannot be spent on the other.
A **bootstrap `config/albums.yaml` cannot declare an invited account**: it carries
password hashes and runs before any mail could be sent, so it keeps creating shared
keys, which is what a fresh installation needs.

## Three facts that shape the rest

1. **`users.password_hash` is `NOT NULL` and stays that way, and "no password" is
   one reserved argon2 hash.** A hash of random bytes thrown away is refused by the
   comparison the route already performs, at the same cost — but nothing can later
   tell it apart from a real password, and two rules below need to: the last-admin
   count and the account list both have to know that an account has no way in. The
   sentinel is therefore a constant, valid argon2 hash declared once and compared
   against, never a random one — and **generated from CSPRNG bytes whose preimage is
   destroyed**, never by hashing a readable literal. A sentinel that is
   `argon2("NO_PASSWORD")` is a password that opens every account holding it, and
   `/auth/login` compares hashes without branching on the sentinel, by design.
   `config.ts` accepts any `$argon2` string from a bootstrap YAML, so it also has to
   refuse this exact one: only invitation creation writes it. No nullable column, no
   rebuild of `users`, no branch in the timing-sensitive path.
2. **A binding is created only by a code entered by its recipient.** No admin route
   may point an account at an existing identity, or an administrator could sign as
   anyone who has ever commented.
3. **No new environment variable.** Nothing here is configured; D78's wiring rule
   has nothing to catch, and `check:specs` will only be looking for routes,
   migrations and module mentions.

## PR 1 — The binding, the invitation and the code sign-in

Server only. Everything below is reachable through the API and through tests; no
screen changes yet.

- [ ] **Migration 18**: `users.commenter_id`, an integer referencing
      `commenters (id)` with `ON DELETE SET NULL`, plus a `UNIQUE` index over it —
      SQLite counts multiple `NULL`s as distinct, which is exactly "many unbound
      accounts, at most one account per person". The column may carry its
      `REFERENCES` clause because its default is `NULL`; this is the same
      restriction met when adding `albums.connection_id`
      ([D260815g](../08-decisions/D260815g-the-single-row-oauth-token-table-becomes-a-table-of.md)).
      Update the migration count in `specs/03-data-model.md` in the same commit —
      `check:specs` compares it with `db.ts`.
- [ ] **The same migration creates `verification_codes`** — `target` (the address,
      `COLLATE NOCASE` like `commenters.email`), `purpose`, `code_hash`,
      `expires_at`, `sent_at`, `attempts`, `username` (the account an invitation
      targets, `NULL` for every other purpose) — primary key `(target, purpose)`,
      **plus a partial unique index on `username WHERE purpose = 'invite'`**. One
      invitation per address is not the invariant that matters; one invitation per
      **account** is. Without the second index, inviting `mamie` at one address and
      then at another leaves two live codes racing to bind the same account, and
      `/invite` with no address has no single row to remint.
      **`username` is `TEXT COLLATE NOCASE`, and references `users (username)` with
      `ON DELETE CASCADE`.** The cascade stops a deleted account leaving its code
      behind, where recreating the same username would let the original recipient
      bind an account that may now be an administrator; renaming is not reachable —
      `ConfigRepo.updateUser` takes no username — so deletion is the whole of it.
      The collation is not decoration: `users.username` is `COLLATE NOCASE`, and a
      child column left on the default would let `Mamie` and `mamie` both satisfy
      the partial index and both be invitations to one account.
      Two `CHECK`s carry invariants nothing else states: one restricting `purpose`
      to `identity`, `signin` and `invite`, and one making `username` non-null
      **exactly when** the purpose is `invite`. Every column but `username` is `NOT NULL` — a composite primary key
      does not impose it in SQLite, where a rowid table accepts a null inside one.
- [ ] **Minting an invitation deletes any invitation matching _either_ axis, then
      inserts — one statement pair, one transaction.** The two constraints refuse a
      duplicate; neither replaces it. Inviting an account at a second address
      conflicts on the partial index, inviting a second account at one address
      conflicts on the primary key, and an upsert can name only one of them. Writing
      "the second replaces the first" without saying how is how this ships as a
      constraint error on the day somebody corrects a typo in an address.
      The migration **drops the four code-state columns from `commenters`**:
      `code_hash`, `code_expires_at`, `code_sent_at`, `code_attempts`. Nothing
      indexes them, so `ALTER TABLE … DROP COLUMN` applies. **`pending_display_name`
      stays**: the rule it enforces is about the name, not about the code —
      requesting a code renames nobody (D38), and dropping it would restore a rename
      this repository already closed. **Pending codes are invalidated by the
      upgrade**, deliberately and not as a side effect: they survive a restart today
      because they live in SQLite, so this is a fifteen-minute cost paid once, at a
      moment somebody chose. The purpose is part of the key rather than a flag, so a
      code minted for one flow is not merely refused by the other — it is not found
      by it.
- [ ] **Each of create, consume, convert and unbind is one transaction with one
      owner, and that owner is not `ConfigRepo.createUser`/`updateUser`.** Both call
      `invalidate()` before returning (`config-repo.ts`), so composing them inside an
      outer transaction leaves the in-memory snapshot rebuilt from uncommitted state
      if that transaction later rolls back — and `PRAGMA data_version`, which is how
      the snapshot notices a change, does not move for a write on its own
      connection. The cache is therefore invalidated **after** the outer commit,
      never inside it. Creating an account by address is the case that shows why it
      matters: the account and its invitation must land together, or an invitation
      that conflicts leaves a sentinel account nobody invited and nobody can enter.
- [ ] **`ConfigRepo`** carries `commenterId` on its snapshot of an account, and
      resolves address → account for the sign-in route. That lookup runs once per
      sign-in and may query SQLite; what must stay in memory is `canSee()`, which
      runs once per thumbnail and is the path nothing here may touch.
- [ ] **The last-admin protection counts administrators who can actually sign in.**
      `adminCount()` (`config-repo.ts:280`) counts rows, and `admin.ts:425` refuses
      to remove the last one. An admin account whose invitation is still pending has
      an unusable password and no binding, so counting it lets the only working
      administrator demote or delete themselves and leave the instance
      administrable by nobody — the exact outcome
      `specs/04-security-and-access.md` says requires shell access to repair. The
      count therefore excludes accounts whose hash is the sentinel and whose
      binding is absent — which is only answerable because the sentinel is a
      constant, and is the reason it is one.
      **The predicate moves with it.** `admin.ts` refuses when the count is one; with
      a usable count, refusing to delete or demote a _pending_ admin while the
      working one remains is the new wrong answer. It guards when the **target** is
      itself usable and would leave none behind. "Usable" is structural — a real
      password, or a completed binding — and deliberately says nothing about SMTP
      being reachable this morning: an instance that turns its mailer off has
      `pnpm reset-password` as the way back, and an account state that changed with
      the weather would be worse than the lockout it tried to prevent.
- [ ] **`plugins/auth.ts`**: the identity comes from `users.commenter_id` when it
      is set, and from `session.commenter_id` otherwise. Both still reread per
      request. Because the column is written only at consumption, **a bound identity
      is always a verified one** — the rule that an unverified identity is attached
      to no session then holds without a check. `SessionUser` gains
      `identityBound: boolean`, which the interface needs in order to stop offering
      to forget an identity the server will refuse to forget.
- [ ] **`POST /api/admin/users` takes exactly one of `password` or `email`.** The
      password path is untouched — creating another shared key must keep working, or
      "nothing changes for an unbound account" is not true. The address path creates
      the account with an unusable hash and sends the invitation; it writes no
      binding and touches no existing `commenters` row.
      `503 mail_not_configured` when there is no mailer, rather than an account
      nobody can enter. Adopting an existing identity is the good case — the
      household member who has already commented keeps their comments — and it
      happens **at consumption**, where the code is what proves the adoption is
      theirs.
- [ ] **`users.commenter_id` is written when the code is consumed, never at
      creation.** This is the item to get right: `commenters.verified_at` proves
      that somebody controls an address, **not** that they accepted this account.
      Binding at creation and adopting an already-verified identity would hand every
      existing session on that account that person's name — an administrator could
      convert an account they hold and sign as anyone who has ever commented,
      without a code ever being entered. The invitation therefore lives as a
      `verification_codes` row of purpose `invite` naming its target account, and
      consuming it is what writes the column.
- [ ] **An invitation outlives a sign-in code.** Purpose decides the deadline:
      fifteen minutes for `signin` and `identity`, **seven days** for `invite`,
      which has to survive somebody's weekend. The number is stated because the
      screen shows the date and a test asserts it. Six digits stay sufficient for the same
      reason D39 gives — the five-attempt ceiling, not the lifetime, is what bounds
      guessing — and a longer life is why that ceiling must be enforced for `invite`
      exactly as for the rest.
- [ ] **Consuming an invitation for an unknown address collects a display name.**
      `commenters.display_name` is `NOT NULL`, the invitation holds an address and a
      username, and nothing else names the person — so the acceptance step asks, and
      the verified identity is created in the same transaction as the binding. This
      is also the right side of D38: somebody's name is theirs to give, never the
      administrator's to maintain. The invitation email itself has no recorded
      locale to go by, since the recipient has never made a request; it falls back
      to the instance default (D260812d).
      **An existing row whose `verified_at` is `NULL` counts as unknown**, and this
      is a security case rather than a tidiness one: `CommenterRepo` creates the row
      and stores the declared name _before_ verification, so anybody behind a shared
      key can pre-seed an address with a name of their choosing. Adopting it as-is
      would bind a real person to a stranger's wording, and would leave
      `verified_at` null under a design that promises every binding is verified.
      Such a row therefore takes the unknown path: name required, `display_name` and
      `verified_at` written, pending name state cleared, binding in the same
      transaction. Only an **already verified** identity ignores `displayName`.
      **`displayName` is optional on `verify`, and its absence does not burn the
      code.** The screen cannot know in advance whether a name is needed — somebody
      typing their own address gets the same indistinguishable `202` whether they
      are being invited or signing in again — so a valid invitation for an address
      `commenters` does not know, arriving without a name, answers
      `display_name_required` **without consuming anything** — which has to be
      stated against the inherited behaviour, not alongside it: `CommenterRepo.verify`
      counts an attempt _before_ comparing, on purpose, so abandoning midway buys no
      free tries. A valid-but-incomplete submission is not an attempt and must not
      increment the counter, or a correct code arriving fifth returns
      `display_name_required` and is exhausted by the time the name comes back. The
      screen asks, and resubmits. A name supplied on any other path is ignored: it must never rename
      an existing identity, which is the rule `pending_display_name` exists for.
- [ ] **`AdminUser` gains what the account list will read**, in PR 1, because PR 1
      owns the API: the bound identity as an address and a name, the pending
      invitation as an address and its expiry, and the four states derived from
      those two. The sentinel hash itself never leaves the server — "no way in" is a
      conclusion the server draws, not a hash the client compares.
- [ ] **`409 identity_taken` names the account holding the address**, and fires
      against **bound** rows only. A pending invitation holds nothing: the primary
      key `(target, purpose)` means one invitation per address at a time, so a
      second one to the same address replaces the first and the account it named
      loses it — which the account list shows, and which is why that column exists.
      An invitation that expires unclaimed frees its address, which is the right
      outcome for a message nobody read.
- [ ] **`POST /identity/request-code` and `/verify` refuse on a bound account**,
      like `/forget`. The identity of such an account is not the session's to
      choose; without this, a bound person declaring another address would attach an
      identity the account contradicts, or silently do nothing.
- [ ] **`POST /api/admin/users/:username/invite` converts an existing account, and
      resends.** With an address it invites that account; without one it mints a
      fresh code for the invitation already pending. It **refuses on an account that
      is already bound** — that would be changing somebody's address, which is out
      of scope, and it is the shape of the impersonation this design exists to
      prevent.
- [ ] **Consuming a conversion code closes the account's sessions, then opens the
      new one, and replaces the password with an unusable hash** — in that order and
      one transaction. Without the closing, the other devices of a household keep a
      session that has just started signing under one person's name; without the
      password, everyone who knew the shared key still walks in under it. It is the
      rule the password-change row of `specs/04-security-and-access.md` already
      states. The ordering is the part that ships broken if nobody writes it down:
      the session being created belongs to the person who just proved the address,
      and a blanket "close every session for this account" run afterwards logs them
      straight back out.
- [ ] **Converting and unbinding also delete this account's approved pairings.**
      Closing sessions is not enough: an approved `device_pairings` row survives it,
      and `claim()` turns a `deviceCode` into a fresh session on that username
      afterwards — a television approved while the key was shared would come back as
      the person it was converted to. The row goes in the same transaction;
      `pairings.ts` needs no new state for this, only to be included.
- [ ] **One rule about passwords, with one exception, both stated exactly.** An
      ordinary password update is **refused** while `commenter_id` is set; the
      single way to give a bound account a password is `{ unbind: true, password }`,
      which in one transaction clears the binding, sets the password and closes the
      sessions. Without the rule, an administrator sets a password on a bound
      account, signs in, and the session signs as that person — the binding they
      were forbidden to assert, entered through the door instead of the window.
      Without the exception, unbinding leaves an account with no identity and a
      sentinel hash that nobody can enter, the administrator included.
- [ ] **The rule lives at the `ConfigRepo` boundary, not on the route**, because
      `scripts/reset-password.ts:48` calls `updateUser` directly. Guarding
      `admin.ts:412` alone leaves a shell command that quietly recreates the
      impersonation, and `reset-password` is precisely the last resort when one
      administrator is locked out. It therefore performs the unbind-and-set for a
      bound account, and says so as it does it.
- [ ] **`POST /api/auth/code/request` and `POST /api/auth/code/verify`**, both
      without a session. `request` mints and sends; **`verify`** is what opens the
      session, and writes the binding when the code consumed was an invitation.
      Failures feed `throttle.ts` on its three axes with the address in place of the
      username.
- [ ] **`request` remints an invitation, not only a sign-in code.** An invited
      address opens no account yet, so a rule phrased around "the address opens an
      account" would answer `202` and send nothing — which is exactly what the
      recipient presses when their code has run out. It therefore looks for a live
      invitation on that target as well, and remints it under the same
      indistinguishable answer and the same one-a-minute delay. **Only a live one**:
      once the invitation has expired and the purge has taken the row, nothing
      anywhere still connects that address to that account, and re-inviting is the
      administrator's to do. The screen says so rather than sending somebody back to
      a button that will silently do nothing.
- [ ] **These two routes say one thing and one thing only, because they are
      public.** `request` answers `202` for an unknown address, for a known one, and
      for one asked again inside the minute alike — it sends or does not send, and
      the response does not say which. `verify` returns a single refusal for
      unknown, mismatched, expired and exhausted alike. `routes/identity.ts`
      distinguishes those four today and may keep doing so: it sits behind
      `requireAuth`, which is the argument `specs/04-security-and-access.md` makes
      for tolerating its `429`. That argument does not survive the move to a route
      anybody can call, and two rapid requests would otherwise be a test for "does
      this address open an account here".
- [ ] **`VerificationCodeRepo` owns the new table**, and `CommenterRepo` loses that
      half: mint, verify, consume, and the rules D39 set, enforced in one place
      instead of once per flow. Five attempts are per code; **one send a minute is
      per address, across every purpose** — `commenters.code_sent_at` was one column
      per person, and a per-row check would let an identity code and an invitation
      reach the same inbox in the same minute, which is the mail-bombing this rule
      exists to stop. **The deadline is per purpose**: fifteen minutes for `signin`
      and `identity`, seven days for `invite`. `routes/identity.ts` moves onto it in the same pull
      request, or the two mechanisms coexist and only one of them is maintained. The
      comment at `packages/e2e/specs/comments.spec.ts:15` names `commenters.code_hash`
      and moves with it.
- [ ] **The hourly housekeeping in `main.ts` purges expired codes**, beside the
      sessions and the pairings. The columns being replaced were overwritten in
      place and accumulated nothing; a table does. The table needs no cap of its
      own: a request for an address nothing knows writes no row.
- [ ] **`POST /identity/forget` returns 409 on a bound account.** Forgetting who you
      are is a shared-key operation; here the way out is to sign out.
- [ ] **Pairing carries the identity on a bound account**, and does not otherwise.
      `pairings.ts` needs no new state — the created session already carries the
      approver's account, and on a bound account that account carries the person.
      **`routes/auth.ts` does need one line**: the successful `/device/poll` reply
      builds its payload inline with `identity: null` hard-coded, so a paired bound
      screen would be cached as unidentified until some later `/me`. It returns the
      bound identity and `identityBound` instead.
      What changes is the paragraph in `specs/04-security-and-access.md`, which
      currently states the opposite without qualification.
- [ ] **The invitation carries a link that pre-fills the address, and nothing
      else.** `PUBLIC_URL/login?email=<address>` lands on the code step with the
      field already filled, and the six digits are still typed. This is the line
      between it and the magic link the sign-in decision rejects: it carries **no
      secret**, authenticates nobody, and grants exactly what knowing the instance
      exists grants. The address in it is the recipient's own and already sits in
      the `To:` header of the message carrying it; `Referrer-Policy: no-referrer`
      keeps it out of any third party's logs. Opening it must **not** mint a new
      code — that would invalidate the one in the message being read — so the step
      offers "send another code" rather than sending one on arrival.
- [ ] **An invitation nobody takes up expires; the account it was for does not.**
      The code dies on its deadline and the purge removes it; the account stays
      until an administrator deletes it, because the alternative is a timer that
      removes album grants somebody set on purpose. What makes that safe is that it
      is visible: the account list shows an account with no way in, and offers
      re-inviting and deleting side by side. Such an account may still hold the
      album grants somebody set on it — what it no longer holds is any credential
      that could exercise them, or any claim on the address.
      **This describes an account created by address, and only that.** A conversion
      keeps its real password until the code is consumed, so an invitation to
      convert that nobody takes up leaves a working shared key exactly as it was —
      the right outcome, and the opposite of the sentence above.
- [ ] **`mail.ts`**: the invitation message and the sign-in message, both following
      `buildVerificationMail`. Each says in its subject and first line what the code
      grants — this is the only defence against the one attack that remains, which
      is social. Keys in **both** catalogues, English first
      ([D260812c](../08-decisions/D260812c-the-interface-is-translated-by-two-typed-catalogues.md)),
      and sent in the recipient's recorded language
      ([D260812d](../08-decisions/D260812d-the-language-travels-in-accept-language-and-is.md)).
- [ ] **Tests**, on invariants rather than on handlers. The four that carry the
      security of this release: **no admin route writes a non-`NULL` `commenter_id`** (invite an
      account naming an address that is already verified, then assert every session
      on it still signs as nobody); **a password cannot be set on a bound account**;
      **the public routes answer identically** for a known address, an unknown one
      and one asked twice inside the minute, and `verify` refuses the four invalid
      cases with one body; **the last usable administrator cannot remove itself**
      while the only other admin account has a pending invitation. Then: an unbound
      account behaves exactly as before; a used code is refused twice; a sign-in code
      is not found by `/identity/verify`; a bound account's identity survives a new
      device without verification; converting closes the sessions, opens the new one
      and leaves the former password refused; unbinding without a password is
      refused; deleting an account removes the binding and closes its sessions.
- [ ] **Two existing assertions break, mechanically and on purpose.**
      `packages/server/test/migrate.test.ts` twice asserts the exact column list of
      `users`, and `specs/03-data-model.md` says a version 3 database gains comments
      "without `users` changing by a single column". Migration 18 changes it by one.
      Both move with it, and migration 18 gets a test of its own: pending codes
      invalidated, the four `commenters` columns gone, both unique constraints, the
      collation, and the cascade.
- [ ] **Both decisions are written into `08-decisions/` by this pull request**, one
      file each, under the identifier of the day they are written — not the day this
      plan was: _an account may be bound to a person, rather than a second
      authentication mode_, and _a bound account signs in with a code sent to its
      address and holds no password_. Their reasoning is above; moving it is the
      last thing this pull request does, and the references in the specs it updates
      point at the files it has just created.
- [ ] **Specs**: `specs/03-data-model.md` (the column, the unique index, the
      migration count, the new table and what the two old ones now are — `users`
      carries authorisation, `commenters` carries identity, which is the sentence
      that stops the next reader wondering why sign-in touches a table called
      `commenters`), `specs/04-security-and-access.md` (the
      binding, the two credentials, the throttle axes reused, the pairing
      qualification, the 409 on forget, what conversion closes),
      `specs/05-api.md` (the three new routes, the `email` field on account

### Two tables PR 1 must not have to invent

**Who owns each transaction.** `ConfigRepo` owns all four, because it owns the
snapshot that must be rebuilt afterwards; `createUser` and `updateUser` are not
reused inside them, for the reason above. Every one invalidates **after** the
commit.

| Operation            | Writes                                                          |
| -------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Create by address    | `users`, `verification_codes`                                   |
| Consume a sign-in    | `verification_codes`, `sessions`                                |
| Consume an invite    | `users`, `commenters`, `verification_codes`, `sessions`         |
| Consume a conversion | the above, plus `sessions` closed and `device_pairings` deleted |
| Unbind               | `users`, `sessions`, `device_pairings`                          |

`pnpm reset-password` calls the unbind owner for a bound account rather than
reaching `updateUser` itself.

**What the public routes answer, and what they count.** A `429` keyed to an
address is an oracle, so `request` counts on the caller's axis alone — and it
counts **every call**, whatever it decided to do.

| Event                                           | Answer                      | Throttle             |
| ----------------------------------------------- | --------------------------- | -------------------- |
| `request`, address opens an account             | `202`, code sent            | `ip` axis            |
| `request`, invitation pending                   | `202`, code sent            | `ip` axis            |
| `request`, address unknown                      | `202`, nothing sent         | `ip` axis            |
| `request`, asked again inside the minute        | `202`, nothing sent         | `ip` axis            |
| `request`, caller blocked                       | `429` + `Retry-After`       | —                    |
| `verify`, unknown / wrong / expired / exhausted | one refusal                 | `fail()`, three axes |
| `verify`, valid but a name is needed            | `display_name_required`     | nothing counted      |
| `verify`, valid                                 | session, like `/auth/login` | `succeed()`          |

**Unconditionally is the load-bearing word**, and counting only what the route
actually sent is the version that looks careful and leaks. The `ip` axis is shared
with `/auth/login`, so an attacker walks it to one below its threshold with failed
sign-ins, then sends a single `request` for a candidate address: if the counter
moved, the address was known. The uniform `202` closes the answer channel, and a
counter that depends on the answer reopens it one call later. So the counter is a
function of **how much the caller asked**, never of what the answers were — which
also still bounds mail-bombing across a thousand addresses, since a thousand calls
trip the axis whether or not anybody exists behind them.

## PR 2 — The two screens

- [ ] **`LoginPage.tsx` offers the address as well as the username**: one field,
      then the six-digit code, on the same screen and without a route of its own. A
      person invited by email has never been told a username. `?email=` fills the
      field and moves straight to the code step **without requesting one**, since
      the reader is holding the code that link was sent with. Accepting an
      invitation asks for a name in the same step, since that is where the person
      first says who they are.
- [ ] **`UsersSection.tsx` and `UserForm.tsx`**: create by address as an
      alternative to create by password, and a column saying which of the four
      states an account is in: a shared key, a person, an invitation still open
      until its date, or **an account with no way in** — the invitation expired and
      nobody took it up. The third is read from the `verification_codes` row, where
      an unclaimed invitation lives; the fourth is the sentinel hash with no
      binding, which is what makes it a state rather than a guess. Re-inviting and
      deleting sit side by side on the row. This column is the only thing that will

- [ ] **Converting an existing account is offered on its row**, through the same

## Open questions, deliberately left open

- **Changing the address of a bound account.** Out of scope for 1.3.0,
  and `/invite` refuses on a bound account precisely so that nobody reaches it by
  accident. Unbinding and re-inviting works and leaves the old signature on old
  comments. If it turns out to matter, the flow is a code to the new address entered
  while signed in — a fourth purpose in `verification_codes`, not a new table.
- **What a person does when they lose access to their address.** Unbinding with a
  new password is the answer the plan implements, and it makes an administrator the
  recovery desk for an account they cannot otherwise enter. Whether that is the
  right amount of ceremony is worth revisiting once somebody has needed it.
